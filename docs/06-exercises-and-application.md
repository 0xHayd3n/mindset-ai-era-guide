## Original worked example: a community help desk

This is a **companion-created hypothetical example**. It is not an organization, case study, protocol, or result reported by the article. Suppose a community service desk wants to help residents reach the right support team. An assistant could draft intake summaries and suggest routing. The pillars can organize questions before building anything.

| Pillar | Application question | Concrete artifact to inspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curiosity | Which missing detail could make the assistant's routing wrong? | An assumption log and deliberately difficult sample requests |
| Purposeful adaptation | What resident need should the redesign serve? | A purpose statement focused on accurate, accessible service |
| Broad systems thinking | Where can a faster intake process move work or risk? | A map of residents, intake staff, specialist teams, appeals, and queue feedback |
| Outcomes | How will the team recognize improvement? | Definitions for total resolution time, routing accuracy, access, and staff workload |
| Collaboration | Which tasks can the assistant propose and which decisions need a person? | A task and review matrix with an escalation owner |
| Stewardship | Who can be harmed by an incorrect summary or exposed information? | Data-handling rules, error correction, a complaint route, and monitoring responsibilities |
| Building | What new arrangement is worth prototyping? | A small reversible workflow with visible human review |

Notice that selecting a tool is only one part of the design. Also notice that this table makes the pillars operational through extra choices. The article does not supply the specific logs, metrics, matrices, or thresholds used here.

## Turn a broad recommendation into a testable question

“A better mindset improves performance” is too vague to evaluate. A narrower companion question is: **does training intake staff to record assumptions and verify source evidence change routing accuracy and review time, relative to the current workflow, on a defined set of requests?** This tests a specified practice, not the entire worldview.

Before any future study, define how requests are sampled, which cases are excluded, what counts as a correct route, who labels it, how privacy is protected, what training differs between groups, and how time is measured. Report errors and costs as well as speed. Tool versions and review effort should be recorded because either can explain an apparent difference. There are no study results in this guide.

## Discussion prompts

1. Which part of pillar 3 is a practical responsibility principle, and which part is a broad capability claim about AI? What evidence would each need?
2. Does the teammate metaphor help people review work, or can it encourage misplaced trust? Give an observable prediction rather than a preference alone.
3. If a process gets faster while appeals become harder, which outcome and stewardship questions were omitted?
4. How would you verify the p. 5 demand statistic? Name the baseline, period, role definition, geography, data source, and counting method you would need.
5. Is a genuinely new product better than improving an existing service? Explain why novelty alone does not settle the value question.
6. What evidence would persuade you that one pillar is redundant or that an important practice is missing?

## Suggested answer checks

A strong answer separates what the author says from what the respondent proposes. It gives a source page for source claims, identifies uncertainties, and avoids treating the hypothetical 60% target or secondary 800% statistic as validation. A strong application also identifies a person or institution that retains responsibility for decisions.

The source itself invites reflection on weak points among the first three pillars in its [conclusions, PDF pp. 4-5](https://biomedgrid.com/pdf/AJBSR.MS.ID.003878.pdf#page=4). Use that as a conversation starter. The article does not provide a validated self-assessment score, hiring test, clinical instrument, or certification scheme.
