# 3. Pillars 4-7: outcomes, collaboration, responsibility, and creation

## Pillar 4: Lead with outcomes, not tools

The author proposes specifying the problem and intended result before selecting technology. He contrasts a vague request for many AI-generated ideas with a prior definition of what a useful idea must accomplish. [Pillar 4 and remark, PDF p. 3](https://biomedgrid.com/pdf/AJBSR.MS.ID.003878.pdf#page=3)

The article illustrates this with a team that states an intake-processing target of a 60% reduction in 90 days. That number is a hypothetical management goal. It is not a measured result, a recommendation backed by a calculation, or an effect of adopting the seven pillars. A goal also needs a quality condition: shorter intake time could be harmful if requests are misclassified or people lose access.

A companion application is to write an outcome statement containing the affected population, desired change, time horizon, and constraints. Then choose an AI task only if it plausibly contributes. The article does not prescribe a formal outcome template or evaluation design; those are useful next steps for applying its advice.

## Pillar 5: Collaborate with AI as a virtual teammate

The article presents collaboration as a division of cognitive work. People provide context, ask follow-up questions, check outputs, and integrate components into the larger project. AI is used for work such as initial drafting, data processing, or simulation. The human role is described as orchestration throughout the process, rather than a cursory inspection at the end. [Pillar 5 and remark, PDF p. 3](https://biomedgrid.com/pdf/AJBSR.MS.ID.003878.pdf#page=3)

The teammate metaphor can make delegation easier to discuss, but it should not obscure who is accountable. A system may produce useful work without understanding a commitment, accepting a duty, or reliably recognizing its own limits. The article's language about shared accountability should therefore not be used to transfer a person's responsibilities to software.

An original practical decomposition is: define the task, state the acceptable evidence, identify forbidden actions, select the reviewer, and decide how uncertain output returns to a person. Those controls are companion suggestions, not an evaluated protocol from the article. Likewise, the mention of running a thousand simulations is illustrative; there is no simulation study, model specification, or result table in this paper.

## Pillar 6: Ethics and stewardship as anchors

The author broadens responsibility beyond avoiding biased data. He asks readers to consider human impact, transparency, responsible management, and longer-term effects. He also emphasizes verifying generated information because outputs can be wrong or fabricated. [Pillar 6 and remark, PDF p. 4](https://biomedgrid.com/pdf/AJBSR.MS.ID.003878.pdf#page=4)

The source explains hallucinations through learned statistical patterns and discusses pragmatics: context, intention, social norms, and background knowledge. Some formulations are categorical, including assertions about what language models do not accomplish. They are explanatory assertions rather than an empirical characterization of all contemporary AI systems. Tools, retrieval, structured inputs, and deployment design can change how a system handles a task; this paper does not test those variations.

The stewardship principle is broader than factual checking. A correct classification could still create an unfair burden or damage a public service when deployed at scale. In the adjacent builder discussion, the author gives employee mental health and public-service disruption as examples of consequences that can undermine apparent efficiency. These are ethical illustrations, not observed harms measured in an experiment in this article.

## Pillar 7: A builder's mindset

The final pillar turns the preceding habits toward creating new structures, products, governance arrangements, or business models. Its emphasis is redesigning the environment in which technology operates, rather than only making the present arrangement faster. The conclusion returns to the human role in selecting purpose and designing what follows. [Pillar 7 and conclusions, PDF pp. 4-5](https://biomedgrid.com/pdf/AJBSR.MS.ID.003878.pdf#page=4)

Creation is not itself evidence of benefit. A prototype can be novel and still fail its purpose. A careful application therefore carries stewardship and evaluation into the building stage. This is a companion inference from the interaction of the pillars, not a demonstrated result.

## Reading the conclusion

The author asks readers to identify their weakest area among curiosity, purposeful adaptation, and systems thinking, then begin changing their own practice. That is a reflection prompt. The paper supplies no scoring instrument, thresholds, or validated personal diagnosis. A reasonable response is to select a concrete behavior to examine, record what changes, and remain willing to revise the framework when observations disagree.
