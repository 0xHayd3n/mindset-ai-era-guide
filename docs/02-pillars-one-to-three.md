# 2. Pillars 1-3: inquiry, direction, and the wider system

[Home](../README.md) · [Previous](01-context-and-concepts.md) · [Next: pillars 4-7](03-pillars-four-to-seven.md)

## Pillar 1: Deep curiosity

The author asks readers to keep investigating an AI answer: why did this result appear, what assumptions support it, and where does it fail? Curiosity becomes an active practice of testing tools and ideas rather than passive consumption. The paper contrasts immediate access to summaries with the continued need to ask worthwhile questions. [Pillar 1 and remark, PDF p. 2](https://biomedgrid.com/pdf/AJBSR.MS.ID.003878.pdf#page=2)

An original way to apply this idea is to keep an assumption log. If an assistant recommends a delivery schedule, record the assumptions about staffing, travel times, access, and customer constraints. Change one assumption and see whether the recommendation changes appropriately. This turns an apparently complete answer into an object of inquiry. It is a companion technique; the article does not supply or evaluate an assumption-log protocol.

The pillar is not a license to regard every surprising output as a discovery. Curiosity also includes checking whether a claim has a source, whether the source says what the model claims, and whether the relevant conditions match the current task. A fluent explanation can still be unsupported. The article connects its inquiry habit to purposeful adaptation: investigating limitations should inform what one changes next.

## Pillar 2: Adaptation with a purpose

The central distinction is between changing methods because a new tool exists and changing methods to serve a durable aim. The author uses biological adaptation as an analogy, then discusses Blockbuster and Netflix and a hypothetical media company. The media example contrasts producing more articles with reconsidering the audience relationship and the role of human writers. [Pillar 2, PDF pp. 2-3](https://biomedgrid.com/pdf/AJBSR.MS.ID.003878.pdf#page=2)

Read these examples as strategic illustrations. The article does not present a historical causal study of the two companies, or an evaluated media-company intervention. Nor does the biological reference demonstrate that a management practice works. An analogy helps frame a question; evidence would be needed to establish the answer.

For a practical reading exercise, separate three items: the purpose, the current method, and a proposed replacement. A team's purpose might be to help people resolve a service problem accurately; its current method might be manual triage; its replacement might be AI-assisted routing. Faster routing is valuable only if it helps the purpose. If routing errors increase, efficiency on one step may be a poor adaptation overall. These are companion examples, not findings reported by Garza Ulloa.

Purpose should also be contestable. Different affected groups may disagree about what value matters. The paper urges a stable direction but does not provide a procedure for resolving conflicting purposes. That gap connects this pillar to stewardship.

## Pillar 3: Think of wide-open on completed systems

The article urges attention to interconnected components, patterns, feedback, and distant consequences. Its contrast is between a bounded optimization task and a world affected by regulation, geopolitical events, social expectations, and changing preferences. [Pillar 3 and remark, PDF p. 3](https://biomedgrid.com/pdf/AJBSR.MS.ID.003878.pdf#page=3)

The important transferable question is: **which consequences did the task definition leave out?** For instance, a faster intake system could move a queue downstream to a smaller specialist team. Its local success could therefore coexist with worse end-to-end service. A systems view expands the measurement boundary before declaring success.

The author makes categorical claims that AI cannot replicate the human strategic role. This guide treats those as his position, not a demonstrated impossibility theorem or model comparison. “Human systems judgment is necessary in this deployment” is a claim that can be made with a defined context and responsibility model; “AI can never perform systems reasoning” is much broader. The article supplies no comparative benchmark to settle that broader claim.

## A joint reading exercise

Choose one generated recommendation. List an assumption to test under pillar 1, the lasting aim under pillar 2, and two affected parties outside the immediate task under pillar 3. Then explain a change you would make after combining all three perspectives. State what evidence could make you reverse that change.

This exercise illustrates the interaction of the pillars. It does not imply that the article establishes a fixed ordering, a necessary-and-sufficient set of skills, or an improvement effect of any particular size.
